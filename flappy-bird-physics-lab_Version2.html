Real-life application: pitch control of aircraft and drones

A flying vehicle has to do two things at once: move its centre of mass (linear motion) and keep its nose pointed where it is going (rotational motion). The game models both.

Linear part. Between flaps the bird is in free fall with a = −g. The flap is an impulse, J = mΔv, much like a drone's propellers or a bird's downstroke giving a short burst of lift. A lunar lander uses the same equations with the Moon's g = 1.62 m/s². Try the Moon preset and watch the rise height grow by 6×.

Rotational part. An aircraft's nose tends to align with its flight path, γ = tan⁻¹(vy/vₓ). An autopilot does this by applying a pitching torque τ = κ(θtarget − θ) − cω, which is a spring (κ) plus a damper (c) acting on the moment of inertia I. This is a proportional-derivative (PD) controller. The game uses the same law.

Tuning matters. From your current sliders:

Engineers usually aim for ζ ≈ 0.7: quick response with little overshoot. Set damping low and the bird wobbles like an aircraft's short-period pitch oscillation. Set it high and the nose reacts sluggishly. This is a simplified model: there is no aerodynamic lift or drag apart from an adjustable terminal fall speed (4.8 m/s by default), and the "spring" stands in for a control system rather than a physical spring.

Equations used

  1. v = v₀ + at,  Δy = v₀t + ½at² constant-acceleration kinematics (m, m/s, s). Free fall: a = g = 9.81 m/s².
  2. x = vₓt uniform motion; distance in m. Here x = ∫vₓ dt because vₓ rises slowly with score.
  3. J = mΔv = Δp impulse–momentum, N·s
  4. W = mg weight, N (kg·m/s²)
  5. τ = Iα,  I = ½mr² rotational dynamics; τ in N·m, I in kg·m², α in rad/s²
  6. ω = dθ/dt,  θ = ∫ω dt rotational kinematics, rad/s and rad
  7. τ = κ(θt − θ) − cω spring–damper torque; κ in N·m/rad, c in N·m·s/rad
  8. ωₙ = √(κ/I),  ζ = c / (2√(κI)) natural frequency (rad/s) and damping ratio
  9. KE = ½mv² + ½Iω²,  PE = mgh energy in J (shown as mJ)

Try this

  1. Press Earth, flap once, and compare predicted and measured rise height.
  2. Press Moon and flap again. Does the error stay small?
  3. Drop damping c to 0.2 mN·m·s/rad and watch θ(t) ring after each flap. Then raise it to 6 and compare.
  4. Crash into a pipe and read ω(t): the bird now spins freely, so its rotational kinetic energy ½Iω² decays only through friction.
  5. Press P to pause, then set m = 0.3 kg and r = 0.12 m. I = ½mr² grows, so the same torque now gives a smaller α and the nose reacts more slowly.
